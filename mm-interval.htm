Interval

"A degree of heterogeneity of material compelled into unity by the operation of the poet's mind is omnipresent in poetry."

—T.S. Eliot, The Metaphysical Poets.

"Interval is the shorthand of God."  —Marshall McLuhan

Some consternation might arise from McLuhan's habit of conflating the parallelisms of figurative language (simile, metaphor, symbolism and allegory) with the components of hybrid art forms (the movie) and hybrid technologies (the telegraph). Figurative language achieves its effects with analogy or the comparison of two disparate terms, a faculty which may well be the foundation of human intelligence. (As McLuhan notes elsewhere, a fish does not understand water unless it has an anti-environment, like land or air, with which to compare it; and human conscious may arise simply from the recognition of differences.)                                                  

Thus when Bobby Burns writes 'My love is like red, red rose,' he is speaking of his beloved in terms of a flower, choosing a vivid simile over a prosaic description. And when Hopkins celebrates the 'Brute beauty and valour' of a windhover buffeted in flight, he is using dramatic voice to symbolize the presence of Christ in nature. In both cases two disparate entities (a lover and a rose, a falcon and Christ) separated by the interval of heterogeneous categories, fuse in a metaphysical frisson, to suggest a transcendent world that subsumes both, bringing about a heightened state of awareness we typically associate with artistic or religious experience.

The use of allegory in the mythological parallelisms of James Joyce's Ulysses, i.e. the juxtaposition of Dublin with ancient Greece, is similar to a voltage potential separated by a gap, and it produces its effect with the involuntary fill-in by human imagination (or what Freud called ‘dream-work’) of the intervening centuries, creating a panorama of historical pageantry spanning time and place. (The same fill-in of interval occurs even when the two parallel terms are products of pure fancy, and the imagination creates an alternate-world that resolves two terms metaphysically, giving 'to airy nothing a habitation and a name.')

Now compare the technique of simile, metaphor, symbolism and allegory with the creation of a hybrid technology like the telegraph, which is the fusion of two parallel media, electromagnetism and print. The evolution of this hybrid involved human imagination and invention, a kind of fill-in. Two disparate terms, electricity and print, thought to belong to different worlds, were combined by a bold act of human imagination. So, though the terms of allegory are esthetic and imaginative (Dublin and Greece), and the terms of the telegraph are material (the electromagnetic telegraph and print), and the products of their respective synthesis are different, a resolution across interval takes place in human imagination, i.e. imagination leaps across two worlds and generates a new universe that encompasses both.

The new social order engendered by telegraphic communication was a remarkable departure from the old; in terms of media study (where everything is a medium for something else), art and technology are cognitively identical. Thus, when McLuhan equates (or conflates) the figurative language of Baudelaire with hybrid technologies like the telegraph, he is being perfectly literal: he is disclosing the fundamental identity and unity of all human endeavor.

Because anything can be a medium for something else, McLuhan's philosophy has been called a field system, one that covers all the departments of human thought. Ontologically speaking, it is difficult to think of a phenomenon that cannot be subsumed under the hierarchy of media, i.e. the concept of containership.
