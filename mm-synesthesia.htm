Synesthesia

It is a well-known fact that we hear with our eyes, i.e. sounds stimulate our visual center, and visual images stimulate auditory imagination. We also feel with our eyes, e.g. the use of visual images like 'pins and needles,' to describe the tingling sensation in the arm when it 'goes to sleep.' In fact, it is impossible to stimulate any one of the senses, touch, taste, smell, sight and sound, without producing corresponding stimuli in the other senses, a phenomenon which may be the very basis for human consciousness; i.e. if a person had only one sense, without the interplay of another, he would in effect be un-conscious.

This is the case with a hotted up visual sense and the mechanical universe corresponding to it, called 'Newton's sleep' by William Blake, and is an operating definition of hypnosis. Moreover, there is an obvious connection between synesthesia and the part played by metaphor in cognition: for example, when a child says 'my arm feels like pins and needles' it is a simile. So, when there are no words in a language to describe the tingling sensation caused by poor circulation or pressure on a nerve pathway, we grasp for images or vocabulary from another area of experience.

Onomatopoeia is the phonation of a word that imitates the sound associated with an action or object, e.g. mumble, murmur, chirp, buzz, another proof of the role of  association, imagination and the interplay of the senses (hearing what we see and seeing what we hear). Sound has an important influence on the morphology of such words as 'shriek'; the word itself is a kind of shriek. German is particularly rich in such words. The German word for spring, Frühling, seems to communicate all the freshness, fecundity, and thrilling, tremulous exhilaration we associate with that season. Translation seems almost superfluous.
