Abstract Finance: The Doom of Western Civilization?

In chapter 11, McLuhan says: "Oswald Spengler's The Decline of the West originated in large part from his concern with the new mathematics. Non-Euclidean geometries, on one hand, and the rise of Functions in number theory, on the other, seemed to Spengler to spell the end of Western man." McLuhan adds: "We need not feel grateful to apocalyptic writers like Spengler, who see our technologies as cosmic visitors from outer space."

But Spengler may have been onto something. Today he would probably have added abstract finance to his list of concerns. The collapse of the financial markets in 2008 could be attributed generally to an unwise reliance on opaque financial instruments like derivatives (e.g. collateralized debt obligations) and credit default swaps.

In a July 2008 article on Finfacts, we wrote that Wall Street contrarian James Grant had said that Wall Street is usually described as an industry, but it shares precious few characteristics with the metal-fasteners business or the auto-parts trade. The big brokerage firms are not in business so much to make a product or even to earn a competitive return for their stockholders. Rather, they open their doors to pay their employees — specifically, to maximize employee compensation in the short run. How best to do that? Why, to bear more risk by taking on more leverage.

Grant cited Morgan Stanley, which had a ratio of assets to equity of 33 times at year-end 2007 from 26.5 times at the close of 2004. In 2007, Morgan Stanley paid out 59% of its revenues in employee compensation, up from 46% in 2004.

They borrow to the eyes and pay themselves lordly bonuses. Naturally — eventually — they drive themselves, and the economy, into a crisis. And to the scene of this inevitable accident rush the government's first responders — the Fed, the Treasury or the government-sponsored enterprises — bearing the people's money.

—Michael Hennigan, Founder and Editor of Finfacts

It is arguable that the sudden spiral into economic chaos began when global markets went off the gold standard. The 2008 banking crisis dealt a severe blow to the prestige free market capitalism around the world, and the dubious role played by governmental oversight and financial regulators, significantly negated the argument for democracy, breathing new life into autocracies like Venezuela, Russia and Iran.
