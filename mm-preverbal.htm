Preverbal Man

"Language extends and amplifies man but it also divides his faculties. His collective consciousness or intuitive awareness is diminished by this technical extension of consciousness that is speech." —Chapter 8, "The Spoken Word"

"It is equally conceivable that the electric extension of the process of collective consciousness, in making consciousness-without-walls, might render language walls obsolescent. Languages are stuttering extensions of our five senses, in varying ratios and wavelengths." —Chapter 13, "Housing"

Nothing better demonstrates the power of media to alter our modes of perception than the exquisite difficulty of explaining how profoundly language influences human thought. It is said that Einstein did not learn to speak until he was five years old. Apparently nonverbal ideation is a more natural cognitive activity than the manipulation of static concepts stored in words, and it is not implausible to conclude that this nonverbal cognitive process was the mode of Einstein's later 'thought experiments.'

Rorty, Davidson and Chomsky would have us believe language is some sort of irreducible activity that anchors thought and that it is the foundation of human consciousness. As Davidson puts it, "speaking a language... is not a trait a man can lose while retaining the power of thought. So there is no chance that someone can take up a vantage point for comparing conceptual schemes by temporarily shedding his own." This dovetails neatly with the pieties of political correctness, for if, as these Marxist thinkers tell us, language is the precondition to human thought, then all that is necessary for man's moral rehabilitation is a program of linguistic hygiene.

In contrast, McLuhan holds that language and cognition are separate; indeed, they must be discrete processes, for thought (nonverbal ideation) is the content of language.

[T]he "content" of any medium is always another medium. The content of writing is speech, just as the written word is the content of print, and print is the content of the telegraph. If it is asked, "What is the content of speech?," it is necessary to say, "It is an actual process of thought, which is in itself nonverbal." —Chapter 1

The spoken word was the first technology by which man was able to let go of his environment in order to grasp it in a new way. Words are a kind of information retrieval that can range over the total environment and experience at high speed. Words are complex systems of metaphors and symbols that translate experience into our uttered or outered senses. They are a technology of explicitness. By means of translation of immediate sense experience into vocal symbols the entire world can be evoked and retrieved at any instant.   —Chapter 6

Let us consider this passage from Victory by Joseph Conrad: 

[The new-born day] sprang upon them in its fullness with a swift retreat of the great shadow when the sun, clearing the ridge, looked down, hot and dry, with a devouring glare like the eye of an enemy. But Heyst... appreciated the prolongation of early coolness, the subdued, lingering half-light, the faint ghost of the departed night, the fragrance of its dewy, dark soul captured for a moment longer between the great glow of the sky and the intense blaze of the uncovered sea.

Who but Conrad could speak of the early morning shade as 'the faint ghost of the departed night,' or of the shadows in swift retreat before devouring eye of their enemy, the sun? One wants to linger over such marvels, wondering how he discovered that morning shade was the spirit of the retreating night.

How did Conrad arrive at such a perception? Did he simply juggle some words until he created an ingratiating image in the mind of the reader. Or did he recall an experience or impression he had felt one morning in the Sunda Islands? Surely it was the latter. He moved from the sensory, non-verbal world of feeling, and translated his sensations into a verbal reference in the form of a metaphorical image. This is the very method of contemporary science and poetry.

The method of invention, as Edgar Poe demonstrated in his "Philosophy of Composition," is simply to begin with the solution of the problem or with the effect intended. Then one backtracks, step by step, to the point from which one must begin in order to reach the solution or effect. Such is the method of the detective story, of the symbolist poem, and of modern science. (p. 59, The Gutenberg Galaxy)

This would suggest the verbal or linguistic world of man is secondary to his intuitive life. In fact, it's not too much to say the vast majority of our poetic perceptions are non-verbal, sensations we feel when (say) we witness a sunrise or sunset, or observe winter light, or smell ozone during a storm. Of course as we grow older (and increasingly literate), the rapt wonder of childhood recedes and the conceptual world begins to supplant the impressionistic one, but such socialization or acculturation is rightly viewed as a deformity, as a kind of corruption. The child is father to the man.
