Archetypes, Symbols, Postures, and Gestures

Freud and Jung

What in the world is an archetype? First of all, it is a type of behavior or person or event. Literally anything at all can be an (arche) type. Secondly, it must be important, for the etymology of the word  arche- (first or chief) type (mould) would seem to indicate that it is something primary, i.e. something overarching and comprehensive. Yet, in point of fact, an archetype is only as important as we want to make it. Things are a bit plastic here, but Carl Jung ruled that for an archetype to be worthy of inquiry it should reveal some basic truth about the human condition.

By contrast Freud studied unconscious 'complexes' (another word for archetypes) or tropisms with an emphasis on their pathology or destructive effect on the host, like scapegoating (the tendency to project blame on others), or Narcissism (the human affinity with self-involvement).

Nothing serves to explain this better than a story, or a personal anecdote. While visiting Cambridge University, I entered a campus pub called The Eagle, famous as the place Crick and Watson had announced the discovery of the double helix. I found myself sitting at a table of Cambridge undergraduates. At the time I was an irrepressible proselytizer for the writings of Marshall McLuhan and would use any pretext to launch into an discussion of his philosophy. So, expanding on one of his themes, I described my experience reading political editorials backwards, and how this stripped away ideological bias and uncovered pure archetypes in every paragraph. As I warmed to the subject, one of the students suddenly lashed out at me, demanding that I define archetype: he left no doubt by his sneering tone he considered me an imposter (not to mention a presumptuous Yankee interloper). But I was baffled by his hostility. This was Cambridge University—if vigorous discussion was not welcome here, then where? I answered with an example: "What you just did—that's an archetype!" He was speechless and embarrassed. And then I understood why. There was an astonishingly beautiful Pakistani girl sitting at the table and, without realizing it, I had been monopolizing not only the discussion—but her attention. And what archetype or trespass had I exposed in this bumptious young undergraduate? Raw uncomplicated jealousy, instinctive competition for the goddess-princess—which is universal enough to please Carl Jung himself.

But what has this to do with symbol, posture and gesture? A symbol is an iconic image that stands for an archetype, e.g. the gold coin Ahab nailed to the mainmast of the Pequod; and a gesture is behavioral shorthand for an archetype or a cluster of archetypes, as when Ahab destroys his navigational instruments, relying on hatred as his metaphysical lodestone. Striking an attitude or posture can indicate a frame of mind: the stark figure of Ahab thumping obsessively across the quarterdeck of the Pequod with his wooden peg leg. Freud believed 'complexes' resided in the 'unconscious;' Jung believed archetypes and myths originated in the 'collective unconscious.' There is probably nothing wrong with seeing the world through the prism of archetype and myth, but strictly speaking they are not essential to our understanding of human nature; and what Jung called the 'collective unconscious,' our 'psychic inheritance,' is just an elaborate explanation for the eternal verities. Complexes, archetypes and myths are at best an intellectual crutch, at worst a distraction; a true understanding of the human heart is intuitive and non-verbal.

A Coat

I MADE my song a coat
Covered with embroideries
Out of old mythologies
From heel to throat;
But the fools caught it,
Wore it in the world’s eyes
As though they’d wrought it.
Song, let them take it
For there’s more enterprise
In walking naked.

—William Butler Yeats
