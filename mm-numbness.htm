Numbness, Autoamputation and Narcosis

Something must be said about the word 'numb.'

Most of McLuhan's analogies are adroit and instructive, but at times they can be downright misleading. The choice of a tactile word 'numb' to describe our blindness to new extensions adds sensory connotations to what is a primarily intellectual process. Of what does this blindness consist? First of all, it is a blindness to something hiding in plain sight and that we use every day. For example, the automobile. Secondly, it refers to a new technology that adheres to us in ways of which we are not aware. In short, it resembles the psychological blindness that Freud spoke of in his investigation of complexes named after Greek Myth, like Oedipus. A complex is a piece of furniture in the unconscious we stumble over repeatedly without understanding why.

When we adopt a new technology, like the motorcar, we are so enamored of its immediate utility we ignore its "transforming power." We seem to lack the will and wisdom to anticipate its long term effects, both on a social and spiritual level. On the social level, the automobile destroyed human scale and community, i.e. the medieval ideal of a city without walls, a harmonious mix of town and country; it gave us instead our brutal freeway cities, resulting in a profound impoverishment of our social and spiritual life. If we were to speed up a video of the evolution of the motorcar, we would perceive the arc of its development and the looming social catastrophe of this technology at once. In the era of plenary retrieval and the electric speedup of information, we are able to experience such an innovation and its consequences instantaneously, just as a visionary artist might. But when a process occurs over protracted period of several generations, it has an aura of historical inevitability about it.

The 2008 collapse of the banking industry is a singular example of narcosis or blindness. The financial world went off the gold standard in 1971 and embraced the instruments of abstract finance, which evolved into sophisticated financial agreements known as derivatives. These new financial tools provided increased liquidity but as unregulated entities increased risk exponentially. The adoption of these opaque instrumentalities was a classic case of willful blindness. By 2007, the financial system was a fragile house of cards, based on mortgaged-backed securities (collateralized debt obligations) leveraged 30 to 1, backed by credit-default swaps. This was the product of greedy bankers and brokers of revered financial institutions, the Noble-track math whizzes on Wall Street, a Congressional Finance Committee dominated by the liberal social engineers who authored and pushed through laws mandating politically-correct subprime mortgages. Only a few lonely voices in the wilderness understood the dangers, but no one listened. Intoxicated with the power of these financial tools, the banking community willfully ignored the warning signs. It is a familiar story: men of action (reckless risk-takers and gamblers), triumphed over men of reflection and common sense, with catastrophic results.

In a sense, our new technologies resemble prostheses. A prosthetic device is inanimate: it provides no feedback for control, and is inherently awkward and unpredictable. Yet, despite this lack of feedback and control, we instinctively adapt to a new prosthesis and internalize it, where it becomes of a piece with our sensorium. This is what McLuhan means by closure. As blindly as Oedipus blundered into tragedy, we integrate a new extension into our sensory network, little heeding its terminal consequences. Another component of the numbness or narcosis is the illusion that our extensions are 'out there' and distinct from us, when in fact they are as intuitive as any of our biological functions; i.e. we perceive them as extrinsic, as existing in spatial isolation, where we have full control of them, when the use of these tools has become automatic. Though these prostheses are insensate, they achieve a high degree of autonomy by colonizing the human nervous system, and because we have become the servomechanisms of these technologies, we operate them on a level of blind instinct.

If you board a bus in downtown Bangkok, you will have your transfer chit or ticket punched in a rather unusual way. The bus conductor will perforate the ticket with the fingernail of his little finger. Bus conductors in Thailand traditionally allow this fingernail to grow to a length of a half inch or so, ostensibly to punch tickets; but this extension of the nail also performs a social function: it is proof to all that he is not a lowly laborer or farmer. This is an excellent example of self-amputation or closure by extension. For this proud talisman renders the conductor all but useless for any manual work. To assume this extension of the nail or talon to be any different than the adoption of a new tool or a technology, would be mistaken; for we are at one with our extensions, however artificial, and as much an any human faculty or organ made by the Lord, they constitute the stuff of our human consciousness and identity.

Other References to Autoamputation in Understanding Media

We are as numb in our new electric world as the native involved in our literate and mechanical culture. (Chapter 1)

Whereas, in an ear culture, the sound of a man's name is the overwhelming fact, as Joyce knew when he said in Finnegans Wake, "Who gave you that numb?" For the name of a man is a numbing blow from which he never recovers. (Chapter 2)

The concept of "idol" for the Hebrew Psalmist is much like that of Narcissus for the Greek mythmaker. And the Psalmist insists that the beholding of idols, or the use of technology, conforms men to them. "They that make them shall be like unto them." This is a simple fact of sense "closure." The poet Blake developed the Psalmist's ideas into an entire theory of communication and social change. It is in his long poem of Jerusalem that he explains why men have become what they have beheld. What they have, says Blake, is "the spectre of the Reasoning Power in Man" that has become fragmented and "separated from Imagination and enclosing itself as in steel." Blake, in a word, sees man as fragmented by his technologies. But he insists that these technologies are self-amputations of our own organs. When so amputated, each organ becomes a closed system of great new intensity that hurls man into "martyrdoms and wars." Moreover, Blake announces as his theme in Jerusalem the organs of perception:

If Perceptive Organs vary, Objects of Perception seem to vary:
If Perceptive Organs close, their Objects seem to close also.
(Chapter 4)

Any invention or technology is an extension or self-amputation of our physical bodies, and such extension also demands new ratios or new equilibriums among the other organs and extensions of the body. (Chapter 4)

The moment of the meeting of media is a moment of freedom and release from the ordinary trance and numbness imposed by them on our senses. (Chapter 5)

Perhaps there is a key to some of these problems in the Freudian idea that when we fail to translate some natural event or experience into conscious art we "repress" it. It is this mechanism that also serves to numb us in the presence of those extensions of ourselves that are the media studied in this book (Chapter 6)

Having extended or translated our central nervous system into the electromagnetic technology, it is but a further stage to transfer our consciousness to the computer world as well. Then, at least, we shall be able to program consciousness in such wise that it cannot be numbed nor distracted by the Narcissus illusions of the entertainment world that beset mankind when he encounters himself extended in his own gimmickry.  (Chapter 6)

An extension appears to be an amplification of an organ, a sense or a function, that inspires the central nervous system to a self-protective gesture of numbing of the extended area, at least so far as direct inspection and awareness are concerned. (Chapter  18)

Radio is provided with its cloak of invisibility, like any other medium. It comes to us ostensibly with person-to-person directness that is private and intimate, while in more urgent fact, it is really a subliminal echo chamber of magical power to touch remote and forgotten chords. All technological extensions of ourselves must be numb and subliminal, else we could not endure the leverage exerted upon us by such extension. (Chapter 30)

The transforming power of media is easy to explain, but the ignoring of this power is not at all easy to explain. It goes without saying that the universal ignoring of the psychic action of technology bespeaks some inherent function, some essential numbing of consciousness such as occurs under stress and shock conditions. (Chapter 30)
