Playing Games

[Middle English playen, from Old English plegian.]

The essence of games is that they are not existential, that they are a form of 'play.' Play in primates is preparation for survival. For example, play amongst lion cubs is obviously a pantomime of the stealth, agility and stalking skill they will need for survival when they grow to adulthood, a rehearsal for the kill.

The word 'play' has two meanings: 

(1) to have fun by doing something that has no serious consequences. This sense of the word is associated with children. When a mother tells her children to go out and play, it is recognized that this is an activity appropriate to children. When we say, 'Don't play around!' we are saying 'Be serious!' 

When I was a child, I spake as a child, I understood as a child, I thought as a child: but when I became a man, I put away childish things.

(2) to play at being something, e.g. play-acting, role-playing, make-believe. Politicians are well-known for play-acting, pretending to be a certain person to satisfy the expectations of his constituents.

Both kinds of play are conscious activities, for the participants are aware their actions have no existential consequences. But when games become the basis for interaction in the social arena, they are a symptom of corruption, an empty set of rules that do not contribute to the welfare of the social order. In this case it is possible for the participants to be unaware they are playing a game. E.g. the hereditary peerage and landed aristocracy of England had become a moribund institution no longer capable of providing effective leadership, i.e. its members were only playing at it. The gentry were an effete social club that no longer served the interests of the community and state.

Social institutions, like the British ruling class, that degenerate into game-playing, are not dissimilar in dynamics to societies blinded by media bias. These too involve a kind of unconscious play-acting and game-playing. A society influenced by a bias like print, or one of the 'arrested societies' (e.g. the Eskimos, Nomads, Osmanlis and Spartans*) is immersed in a rule-bound environment or way of life it supposes to be universal, i.e. a society where it was possible 'to feel that the metaphysics of human welfare were being energetically pursued'––when in fact such a society is playing a game with itself. A society based on laissez faire economics is playing such a game. Proponents of free market capitalism, like Ayn Rand, assume that it is self-correcting machine of wealth creation, even though it regularly spirals out of control like a spinning top that has lost gyroscopic stability.

Since play is not goal-directed, it has some indirect practical benefits. The first and most obvious is a relief from the anxiety of the work place and from personal responsibility. The second is that it enables us to see solutions not obvious in a goal-directed setting. Play is the medium of idle fancy and creative problem-solving.

* Eskimos and Nomads, Osmanlis and Spartans have two features in common: specialization and caste. (In the former pair, dogs, reindeer, horses, and cattle supply the place of the human slave castes of the 'Osmanlis.) In all these societies the human beings are degraded by specialization as boat-men, horse-men or warrior-men to a subhuman level in comparison with the all-round men, the ideal of Pericles' funeral speech, who alone are capable of achieving growth in civilization. These arrested societies resemble the societies of bees and ants, which have been stationary since before the dawn of human life on Earth. They also resemble the societies portrayed in 'Utopias'. A discussion of 'Utopias' follows, in which it is shown that 'Utopias' are generally the products of civilizations in decline and are attempts, in so far as they have a practical programme, to arrest the decline by pegging the society at its actual level at the moment. 

[Arnold Toynbee, A Study of History]
