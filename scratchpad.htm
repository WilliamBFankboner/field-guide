In the first introduction to UM, McLuhan mentions the 'consternation of one of the editors' of Understanding Media:

He noted in dismay that "seventy-five per cent of your material is new. A successful book cannot venture to be more than ten per cent new."

Where did all this 'new' material come from? It is apparent from the final sentence of The Gutenberg Galaxy that McLuhan considered it and Understanding Media as companions pieces, parts of a two-volume work, to be read in sequence, rather than as separate standalone works. Most of McLuhan's ideas can be traced to authorities he acknowledged and quoted in The Gutenberg Galaxy. This is not to say these concepts had not already been percolating in McLuhan's mind prior to his encounter with these scholars: the history of ideas is rife with mutual discovery and most intellectual breakthroughs have been made independently and simultaneously by more than one thinker. Or as William Saroyan put it, "The greatest performances are never seen, and never even suspected as actual events."

The fact that so much of McLuhan's book is a synthesis of other thinkers may account for his insistence some tendentious ideas, e.g. the power of TV and print media to directly re-arrange our 'private sense ratios,' (when it seems far more likely that our bias for visual linear order and 'the fiction of  homogeneous particles' is brought about by indirect macro-level influences, e.g. 'patterns of communal interdependence' and other macro phenomena like our global economic infrastructure and the scientific revolution). McLuhan did not know his own strengths: metaphysical vision applied to the modern world. That is why he may have felt the need to develop and originate some ideas of his own.
