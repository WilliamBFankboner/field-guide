McLuhan and Woody Allen in Annie Hall

P r e f a c e

Suppose he is what he sounds like, the most important thinker since Newton, Darwin, Freud, Einstein and Pavlov. What if he is right? —Tom Wolfe

In the introduction to the first edition of Understanding Media Marshall McLuhan observed that little consideration had been given 'the psychic and social consequences' of man's extensions, and that how little could be discerned in a remark made by one of the editors of the book:

He noted in dismay that seventy-five per cent of your material is new. A successful book cannot venture to be more than ten per cent new.

What we do not learn from this remark is that most of this new material was the product of other authors. It was not that McLuhan was claiming credit for the work of others—no scholar has been more scrupulous and gracious in acknowledging his sources than McLuhan—but that he had already documented the authorship of this material in The Gutenberg Galaxy, a work of prodigious scholarhip that preceded Understanding Media. Ideally, one should read the books in sequence, but The Gutenberg Galaxy is such a challenging work in terms of complexity and shear detail, that reading it would be beyond the scope of any but the most dedicated readers.

Moreover, some ideas that are identifiably McLuhan's have not withstood the test of time. One of these, the fundamental theme of The Gutenberg Galaxy, is that the phonetic alphabet and the Gutenberg press have directly altered Western man's perceptions of the world around him, i.e. induced psychic changes in his sensory life that have given him his science and technology, but that have burdened him with a spiritually impoverished view of reality.

Another idea advanced in Understanding Media is that the low definition 'mosaic mesh' of the standard TV image (HDTV was not available at the time) induces in-depth participation by prompting viewer 'fill-in,' i.e. confers a magical enrichment of human consciousness. This is the converse of the GG theme, that two high definition extensions, the phonetic alphabet and Gutenberg press, have profoundly altered the ratio of the human 'sensorium' and brought about the adoption by Western society of Euclidian space and a clockwork universe. The contrast of high and low definition media is a constant theme in McLuhan's writing, and falls under his overarching concern with the psychic effects of man's extensions.

But there is a general agreement that McLuhan's emphasis on the affects of the alphabet and Gutenberg press on sensory organization, as opposed to social organization, was unsound. Nor was it supported by his colleague, Harold Innis. According to James W. Carey:

Both McLuhan and Innis assume the centrality of communication technology; where they differ is in the principal kinds of effects they see deriving from this technology. Whereas Innis sees communication technology principally affecting social organization and culture, McLuhan sees its principal effect on sensory organization and thought. McLuhan has much to say about perception and thought but little to say about institutions; Innis says much about institutions and little about perception and thought.

If, as will be shown in this field guide, McLuhan's fixation on the intense visual conditioning of high definition media like the phonetic alphabet and Gutenberg press, and tactile boost of low definition media like TV and manuscript culture, two of his most important themes, was flawed, what exactly is his achievement?

For disciples of McLuhan, his aperçu and insights were (however dazzling) secondary to the mode of thought he employed to organize them. He was deeply committed to the maxim that 'any study in depth is a study in breadth.' But while a stalwart traditionalist and a Thomist in his approach to God and the Creation, he embraced a system of provisional 'probes' when it came to human perception. In Understanding Media, he integrated the scholarship he had documented in The Gutenberg Galaxy into a coherent metaphysical system, employing 'the technique of insight,' i.e. keeping as many ideas in play at once in order to perceive the 'principles and lines of force' between them in a 'total field of inclusive awareness.'

Like other great minds (Kant, Einstein, Freud) McLuhan was a consolidator of ideas, writing in The Gutenberg Galaxy:

To follow the contours of process as in psychoanalysis provides the only means of avoiding the product of process, namely neurosis or psychosis.

Following Freud's pioneering lead, he opened up the field of popular culture to rigorous analysis: advertising, news, entertainment (TV, movies, comic strips, and comic books), discerning in detective fiction an illustration of 'negative capability.'

Freudian in technique, McLuhan set about psychoanalyzing the effects, often unconscious, of man's extensions and technologies on human perception and on society, and organized his ideas into a unified field system under metaphysical direction. In this, he exceeded Freud, for he showed that human thought consisted of a hierarchy of media (theoretically anything can be a medium), and that no medium [had] its meaning or existence alone, but only in constant interplay with other media. A perceptive psychologist, McLuhan had come closer than anyone before him to defining the terms, operation, and architecture of human consciousness. And it is for this that he must be ranked among the world's great thinkers.

Also like Freud, McLuhan borrowed freely from Greek mythology to illustrate his ideas and perceived a relationship between a society's 'Narcissus attitude' and 'narcosis' (from narke, the Greek word for numbness), i.e. the sense that the extensions of our own bodies [are] really out there and really independent of us… Narcissus was so consumed with his reflection, and numbed to the external world, he was unaware of the ways in which his self-image (individual identity) was constituted of, and completed by, his environment.

The Design of This Field Guide

It has long been a custom of mine to take notes of the books I read on the flyleaves at the back, and usually the page or two which the publisher thoughtfully provides, presumably for this purpose, is ample.... In McLuhan's case I find I have not only covered all the flyleaves provided, but my notes have spilled over into an assortment of airline menus and hotel stationary.  —Kenneth E. Boulding

To students of Marshall McLuhan who have experienced the frustration of scribbling illegible notes in the narrow margins of a paperback, the rationale for a field guide to Understanding Media will be obvious. It is one of those works that ignites commentary, prompting a return to the medieval tradition of manuscript culture (the 'arts of gloss and marginal illumination') and it would be difficult to find of a more flexible medium for entering marginal glosses and the simulation of manuscript culture than an HTML document. The template below consists of a simple two-column table:

Ironically the feminist movement liberated women from the slavery of housework only to shackle them to the dreary routines of the office (the sacrosanct domain of men) adding more automatons to the ranks of 'homogenized Dagwoods.' Yeats presents Locke, the philosopher of mechanical and lineal associationism, as hypnotized by his own image. The "garden," or unified consciousness, ended. Eighteenth-century man got an extension of himself in the form of the spinning machine that Yeats endows with its full sexual significance. Woman, herself, is thus seen as a technological extension of man's being.

The annotations in the left column are provisional glosses of my own. Highlighting can be distracting to readers, especially when it is someone else's, so I have tried to use it sparingly. Highlightings are a lot like scent-markings, a staking out of territory: lime denotes an interesting passage, yellow a central theme. This field guide began life as a self-help project. While it is entirely possible others may find it useful, I have tried to observe a strict discipline in interpreting McLuhan's book: to write exclusively for myself; for I noticed that as soon as I began to think of these notes as product (rather than process), the adventure of free association failed and I lost touch with the material.

This is an approach I can recommend to others with some confidence, i.e. to commune with, and meditate over, each paragraph of each chapter in an open-ended process of discovery, recording insights in this template. (Nota bene: inserting text into this hypertext template requires a basic HTML editor.) Understanding Media is over 300 pages long. If one were to spend a day reading, researching, and annotating one page, one could complete the book in less than a year, a course of study arguably equivalent to a liberal education, in quality of insight, if not quantity of material covered. But one never really completes reading Understanding Media. It is like of a seminal religious document, e.g. The Upanishads, or a revolutionary scientific theory (Wolfe mentioned Darwin and Einstein), that expands infinitely from within, yielding fresh insight and wisdom with each rereading. Indeed, it would be no exaggeration to say there are passages in Understanding Media one could ponder productively over a lifetime.

In some cases, when the left column was too cramped for an extended discussion, I created a popup window actuated by a button click (the 'Sleep States' button above). Popups require only that the user create a separate HTML file and insert the file name (in this case, mm-sleep.htm) into the following source code:

<a HREF="mm-sleep.htm" onClick="return popup(this, 'notes')">
<input type="button" value="Sleep States"></a>

This routine calls a Java script named 'popup' with the file name (this) as an argument. (This is actually easier than it looks, but if you find this level of detail onerous you may prefer to use a simple hyperlink instead of a popup window.)

(Nota bene: these pop-up windows do not stay on top, so be sure to properly close them after use; otherwise the old popup will disappear behind any other windows you happen to have open.)

Reading long documents from a computer monitor can be tedious. Given a choice, most of us would rather curl up with a book: this is as good a place as any to declare that this field guide is intended as only a companion to the printed editions of the book, not as a substitute for the hardcopy (Understanding Media is copyrighted material). The advantage of the hypertext format is that it provides a dynamic template for annotating the text in the Patristic tradition of the ancient church fathers and scholastic manuscript culture, and enables readers to search, index and cross-reference the book's far-ranging topics.

McLuhan lived in a world, the pre-Gutenberg world, in which such things as copyright and intellectual property were non-existent. To quote a passage from The Gutenberg Galaxy:

We are guilty of an anachronism if we imagine that the medieval student regarded the contents of the books he read as the expression of another man's personality and opinion. He looked upon them as part of that great and total body of knowledge, the scientia de omni scibili, which had once been the property of the ancient sages.

It is worth remembering that Understanding Media is itself only a road map to personal enlightenment. No thinker in recent memory has provided us with better tools for understanding the world around us than Marshall McLuhan, but at some point we must abandon this verbal scaffolding and come to terms with experience at an intuitive level.

This is a strategy McLuhan himself would have approved. For it is clear he considered language, and the written word in particular, a mere virtualization of, and ultimately a barrier to, a more inclusive and deeper kind of non-verbal thought. In Chapter 6, Media as Translators, he writes:

The power of technology as dependent on alternately grasping and letting go in order to enlarge the scope of action has been observed as the power of the higher arboreal apes as compared with those that are on the ground.

In terms of Understanding Media, this means the power to grasp and internalize these ideas is only a preliminary step, and that at some point we must 'let go' of this linguistic virtualization, abandoning words and concepts altogether, and seek understanding independent of the artifice of language.

Possibly because it was a species of the classification and fragmentation of knowledge he deplored, McLuhan did not index his books; in fact in The Mechanical Bride he scorned Dr. Hutchens' Great Books project. For he believed that to mechanically index ideas as topics was to put the supremacy of technique [over] nutriment. McLuhan was a firm believer in the part played by interval in human understanding and held that such a specialist approach to his ideas would isolate and strip them of their rich elliptical associations. We find a more elaborate explanation of his method in Chapter 2, Media Hot and Cold, where he speaks of 'concentric patterns':

Instead of static solutions, McLuhan offers us a cognitive tool called a 'probe,' for he believed that throughout the history of metaphysics there had been a fatal tendency to come too quickly to the point, to accept reductive explanations of the phenomenal world that robbed it of its rich complexity and 'multi-dimensional resonance.' Instead of this linear method, the goal should be to keep many ideas in play at once to perceive the lines of force between them. An example of this new way of of thinking can be found in Chapter 9. Analyzing the alphabet technologically, historically, culturally and psychologically, he provides us with compelling proof of his maxim that 'any study in depth is a study in breadth.'

Without question the world we inhabit is more complicated than the world of our ancestors. But it is also an interconnected world, a universe in which the instantaneous retrieval of information, provided by the computer and other electronic technologies, places us in a position to understand incomparably more about it and ourselves than was possible in the preceding industrial age.

Once, when explaining his method of musical composition, Mozart wrote:

the whole, though it be long, stands almost finished and complete in my mind, so that I can survey it like a fine picture, or a beautiful statue at a glance. Nor do I hear in my imagination the parts successively, I hear them all at once. What a delight this is! All this inventing, this producing, takes place in a pleasing, lively dream.

It is impossible to comprehend Understanding Media by listening to its parts successively. Like Mozart, we must survey the book at a glance and hear the music all at once, i.e. access eternity in the moment. It is hoped that the use of glosses, commentary, and notes, employed in this field guide will enable readers to 'hear' all the parts of Marshal McLuhan's compelling message at once, in a 'total field of inclusive awareness.'
