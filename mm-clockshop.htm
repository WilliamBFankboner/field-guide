A Meeting in a Clock Shop - The Rags of Time

One of the most striking scenes in the literature of revolution is found in André Malraux's novel, Man's Fate, wherein a council of Communist commissars and generals meet in a clock shop to plot military strategy. The weird juxtaposition of the mechanically ticking clocks and the counsel of war resonates with illusive irony. Rather than abetting the commissars in their pursuit of Marxist paradise, the gently ticking clocks seem to be mocking the earnest utopian dreamers. Time passes slowly for the revolutionary man of action and the social engineer because their minds are mechanical and sequential, and focused on a narrow materialistic point of view; while time expands to infinity for the man of reflection who meditates on existence and the human condition.

In the Communist strategy session the ticking clocks measure Newtonian or 'profane' time, i.e. time that is fragmented into discrete segments. The revolutionaries march to their goals in finite steps, measured by a tick of the clock, and like Zeno's traveler they never reach their objective because 'sacred' time is dynamic. Or as John Donne expressed it:

Love, all alike, no season knows nor clime,
Nor hours, days, months, which are the rags of time.

Perhaps this is the essential criterion differentiating the conservative from the liberal mind, the capacity to excavate eternity in the moment, i.e. in sacred time. The conservative is spiritually awake to the existential immediacy and all-inclusive nowness of the moment, i.e. he is completely immersed in the metaphysics of being. Conversely, the progressive, with his utopian vision of better things to come, is always on the cusp of a metaphysical epiphany, looking forward to, and preparing to live in, a splendid Eden just over the horizon, contemplating a millennial future that never arrives. Are not the ticking clocks reminding the impatient utopians that the Kingdom of Heaven is eternal?
